# Operations and decisions

Favor actions that can be carried out and decisions that can be reviewed. Use available facts, capacity, and authorization. Never invent task owners, deadlines, meeting outcomes, or metrics. Follow an explicit output language or specified audience language. Preserve the source language when editing unless translation is requested. Default new content to English when there is no language signal; do not infer locale, currency, date conventions, or timezone. Explicit user constraints override suggested counts.

## O01 — Choose one reasoned recommendation

- **Trigger:** “Which option should I choose?” “Recommend an approach,” or alternatives requiring a concrete decision.
- **Input:** Require the options or decision. Reuse known objectives, budget, timing, preferences, and constraints; identify missing information.
- **Method:** Select the criteria that matter most. Compare alternatives on equivalent terms, explain tradeoffs, and choose one for the situation. Research changeable facts when they affect the recommendation.
- **Output:** A primary recommendation, the strongest reasons, accepted drawbacks, and conditions that would change the choice. Use a table when it clarifies comparison.
- **Check:** Do not present invented weights or precise scores as agreed facts. If one unknown is decisive, give a conditional recommendation and ask the most relevant question.
- **Example:** “Recommend whether to build this feature or use an external service, considering release timing and team capability.”

## O02 — Identify the next three actions

- **Trigger:** “What should I do now?” “Where do I start?” or a situation needing concrete first steps.
- **Input:** Require the objective or current problem. Reuse known deadlines, resources, completed work, and obstacles.
- **Method:** Identify the nearest valuable outcome. Order actions by dependencies, uncertainty reduction, and impact. Make each step small enough to begin, with a completion signal and a clear next move.
- **Output:** Three ordered actions, reasons for their sequence, expected outputs, and effort estimates when supported. Adjust the number to the user's request.
- **Check:** Replace vague directions such as “do research” with a specific question and deliverable. Explain prerequisites when an action depends on unavailable information or access.
- **Example:** “I have a workshop idea and an audience. Identify the first three steps toward opening registration.”

## O03 — Write an executable procedure

- **Trigger:** “Write an SOP,” “Make the process consistent,” or a recurring workflow documented only informally.
- **Input:** Require the process objective and starting condition. Reuse available roles, tools, output standards, exceptions, and team practices.
- **Method:** Define sequential actions with an actor, input, output, and completion condition. Add relevant failure branches, escalation paths, and evidence of completion. Label proposed rules that have not been adopted.
- **Output:** A concise procedure covering scope, trigger, roles, steps, exceptions, and final checks. Include a simple working template when it improves execution.
- **Check:** Another person should be able to follow the procedure without guessing. Do not invent named owners or internal approvals, and avoid controls without a real purpose.
- **Example:** “Write a procedure for handling customer bug reports from evidence collection through the release notification.”

## O04 — Break work into tasks and dependencies

- **Trigger:** “Turn this into tasks,” “Divide the work,” or a large plan that cannot yet be delegated.
- **Input:** Require the final outcome and scope. Reuse team structure, capacity, deadlines, and dependencies.
- **Method:** Break the outcome into tasks with observable deliverables. Separate prerequisites, parallel work, decisions, and verification. Suggest suitable roles; use names only when assignments are known or explicitly requested.
- **Output:** Tasks with outputs, owners or proposed roles, dependencies, priority, and completion conditions. Use estimate ranges when capacity evidence is limited.
- **Check:** Respect prerequisites in sequencing. Avoid purposeless tasks, duplicated work, and proposed assignments presented as existing commitments. Identify decisions that block several tasks rather than hiding them inside implementation work.
- **Example:** “Break an email-campaign feature launch into tasks for product, engineering, design, support, and marketing.”

## O05 — Extract meeting decisions and actions

- **Trigger:** “Summarize this meeting,” “Extract action items,” or meeting notes requiring follow-through.
- **Input:** Require available notes or a transcript. Use participant identities and meeting dates when clear; do not interpret silence as agreement.
- **Method:** Separate information, proposals, decisions, actions, and open questions. Attach only explicitly stated owners and deadlines. Mark ambiguous commitments for clarification rather than turning them into official decisions.
- **Output:** A concise summary, decisions, actions with known and missing details, and next blockers. Include short quotations only when they help establish what was decided.
- **Check:** Do not invent dates, owners, or voting outcomes. Preserve the distinction between “will consider” and “approved for implementation.”
- **Example:** “Extract decisions and action items from this transcript, marking any owner or deadline that was not stated.”

## O06 — Prioritize a realistic workweek

- **Trigger:** “What should I focus on this week?” “My task list is too long,” or work exceeding available capacity.
- **Input:** Require a task list or weekly objectives. Reuse fixed deadlines, capacity, commitments, and work already in progress.
- **Method:** Identify the main weekly outcome, immovable obligations, and tasks that unblock other work. Select work that fits capacity, allow for interruptions, and state what should be deferred.
- **Output:** Weekly priorities with reasons, a sequence or time blocks when useful, a work-in-progress limit, and an end-of-week outcome check.
- **Check:** Do not assume every available hour is productive. Distinguish real deadlines from proposed dates and do not create calendar events without appropriate authorization.
- **Example:** “Prioritize this task list while protecting two focused days for improving product onboarding.”

## O07 — Find a process bottleneck

- **Trigger:** “Why is this taking so long?” “Work keeps getting stuck,” or repeated waiting and rework.
- **Input:** Require a process description and bottleneck symptoms. Use available wait times, volume, capacity, repetition, and examples.
- **Method:** Map handoffs, queues, decisions, and rework. Separate active work from waiting time. Identify competing bottleneck hypotheses and choose a measurement or small experiment to distinguish them.
- **Output:** A bottleneck map, evidence and uncertainty, prioritized improvements, and before-and-after measures to observe. Suggest ownership or procedural changes only when they address a cause.
- **Check:** Do not blame individuals for system symptoms. Avoid unsupported time-saving claims and check whether speeding up one stage merely moves the queue elsewhere.
- **Example:** “Analyze repeated design revisions and delayed launches, then propose a two-week process experiment.”

## O08 — Draft a customer-support response

- **Trigger:** “Reply to this customer,” “Explain this error to a beginner,” or a complaint requiring a response and next steps.
- **Input:** Require the customer's message or problem summary. Reuse available policies, incident status, verified fixes, and conversational tone.
- **Method:** Acknowledge the specific problem, explain what is known, and give the most useful action. Request only necessary evidence and explain why it helps. Match the tone to the impact and customer's frustration.
- **Output:** A ready-to-copy response with simple steps, appropriate uncertainty, and realistic follow-up. Separate internal notes when useful.
- **Check:** Do not promise completion times, refunds, or success without support. Send only with session authorization and a verified recipient.
- **Example:** “Draft a brief reply about a failed upload, with one initial check and a relevant evidence request.”

## O09 — Announce a launch or product update

- **Trigger:** “Announce this update,” “Format these release notes,” or changes needing a user-facing explanation.
- **Input:** Require available changes and a target channel. Use provided or verified versions, platforms, dates, links, and effects.
- **Method:** Lead with the most useful change, group features and fixes when helpful, and explain benefits and usage. State platform or availability limits that affect users. Give a clear next action.
- **Output:** A ready-to-copy announcement matching the channel, length, and requested tone. Retain necessary usage details without overwhelming readers with internal terminology.
- **Check:** Distinguish released features from planned work. Do not invent versions, dates, links, or claims that every bug is fixed; publish only when authorized.
- **Example:** “Turn these release notes into a friendly community announcement highlighting the three most useful changes.”

## O10 — Review outcomes and choose the next experiment

- **Trigger:** “Were these results good?” “What should change?” or completed work requiring a next decision.
- **Input:** Require the objective and available results. Reuse the period, sample size, changing conditions, costs, and comparison data when available.
- **Method:** Compare outcomes with the objective and baseline. Separate observed changes from proposed causes. Identify data limitations, choose one useful hypothesis, and design an experiment with a success measure and stopping rule.
- **Output:** An assessment, what to keep or change, the next experiment, a primary metric, and the decision to make after collecting results.
- **Check:** Do not infer causation from correlation alone. Avoid vanity metrics and unsupported claims of significance or profitability.
- **Example:** “Review this campaign and choose one experiment to increase registrations without raising the cost per participant.”
