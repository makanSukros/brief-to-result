# Hook Lab: sales and affiliate openings

Use for deliberate BTR requests for sales hooks, affiliate openings, product-video intros, ad headlines, or stronger opening lines. This is an agent-run creative method, not a fill-in-the-blank generator or a claim of higher conversion. Keep the 80 core workflows; the 24 H-series formulas are additional creative recipes, not 24 new general workflows.

Read this guide, then only the relevant families in [hook-formulas.md](hook-formulas.md). See [hook-examples.md](hook-examples.md) for fictional demonstrations and acceptance cases. Ordinary users only need to ask: `@btr write 10 affiliate hooks for this product`. Do not require them to name Hook Lab, memorize H IDs, provide a JSON file, or complete a marketing questionnaire.

## 1. Build from the actual product, not from a famous sentence

Extract what is available: product/function, audience, daily situation, real friction, desired change, mechanism, proof, price/conditions, channel, creator's actual experience, language and tone. Use selected profiles under the existing freshness rules. A request in Indonesian with Indonesian hook examples establishes an Indonesian output context unless the user asks otherwise; the documentation stays English-first. Do not infer an Indonesian market from an unrelated example alone.

If the product and function are missing, ask one short question: "What product is this for, and what does it do?" If a product link or screenshot is supplied, inspect it using available tools; distinguish observed details from seller claims. Do not invent hidden specifications from appearance. If access fails, request the relevant information and complete unaffected work. A known product with sparse information can still receive situation-led hooks; do not invent a price, measured benefit, popularity, or first-person review to fill gaps.

For each usable capability, make this small map internally:

`observable feature -> action it changes -> recognizable situation -> reason to care`

For a cable holder: `holds a suitable cable end at the desk edge -> less reaching under the desk -> phone needs charging during work -> less interruption`. For software: `saved report template -> fewer repeated setup steps -> the same weekly report -> less re-entry`. The proposed emotional angle is not evidence of an actual time saving.

Keep product facts, seller assertions, working creative assumptions, and unknowns distinct. Fictional demonstration facts must stay labeled as fictional outside the copy. Do not transfer example prices, functions, or customer stories to a real product.

## 2. The composition rule

**Recognizable moment + one tension/contrast + a specific reason to keep watching -> an honest product payoff.**

This is a flexible design test, not a compulsory four-clause sentence. A visual can carry the situation while the spoken line carries the contrast. Use one main idea per hook. Two short beats often read better than an overloaded sentence. A question mark, capital letters, ellipses, and an exclamation do not create an idea by themselves.

Choose a distinct emotional route: recognition, curiosity, relief, playful frustration, surprise, skepticism, identity, or buying confidence. Tie it to a visible action or a real decision. Do not make every product a life transformation.

Prefer `Where did the cable go this time?` with a cable reaching shot over `You won't believe this amazing little gadget!`. Prefer a precise tradeoff over an unsupported superlative. Avoid opening every candidate with "Did you know", "Turns out", "This little thing", "Tahukah kamu", or "Ternyata".

## 3. Select a strategy, then choose a voice

Use the formula families as different persuasive mechanisms:

| Families | Core move | Typical situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H01-H04 | Recognizable friction | The viewer has not connected a small nuisance to a solution |
| H05-H08 | Contrast and reframing | Attention needs a surprising but relevant turn |
| H09-H12 | Demonstration and discovery | A visible mechanism can carry the argument |
| H13-H16 | Fit, value, and objections | The viewer is evaluating whether to buy |
| H17-H20 | Experience and proof | Genuine review notes or comparable evidence exist |
| H21-H24 | Participation and next-step relevance | A useful choice, situation, or confirmed offer can prompt action |

Voice is a separate layer: conversational, direct, playful, understated, premium, skeptical, warm, or regional. Changing language or slang alone does not make a different angle. "Roast" the awkward situation or mismatch, not a person's worth, body, finances, ethnicity, or other sensitive characteristic. Do not use identity pressure such as implying someone is unintelligent unless they buy.

For requested regional wording, use the user's examples as a register reference, not as text to recycle. Prefer a light, natural touch; do not mix unrelated dialects or pretend fluency when unsure. Regional wording is optional and must not become the global default. Keep jokes understandable to the stated audience. A native-speaker review may be appropriate for polished regional campaigns.

## 4. Generate diversity before polishing

Honor the requested count and scope. Without a count, a useful starting point is six distinct hooks; this is a configurable default. For a batch of ten or more, aim for at least five different mechanisms when the product facts support them, and avoid putting more than roughly a third into one family. Do not force unsupported proof-led families to satisfy a quota.

Vary at least two dimensions across neighboring hooks: situation, mechanism, emotional route, point of view, sentence rhythm, objection, or visual reveal. "Small product, big benefit" rewritten ten times is one idea, not ten. An aggressive adjective or an emoji is not an additional angle.

Privately shortlist and revise the candidates. Do not expose private internal reasoning or an elaborate scoring sheet. Apply these checks:

1. **Product specificity:** replace the product with an unrelated item. If the line still works unchanged, add a concrete behavior, object, situation, or visual dependency.
2. **Recognizability:** can the intended viewer picture the moment immediately? Avoid claiming that everyone experiences it.
3. **Tension:** is there a real question, mismatch, interruption, or decision, rather than empty hype?
4. **Payoff:** can the next shot or sentence answer the promise using the actual product?
5. **Spoken naturalness:** read it aloud; shorten stiff clauses and avoid forced slang. Measure real voice-over duration when timing is strict; do not claim every line fits three seconds.
6. **Distinctness:** compare mechanisms, not just wording. Replace near-duplicates and overused opening stems.
7. **Claim eligibility:** every factual, numeric, comparative, or experiential assertion must have a valid basis.

Use pass/revise/block judgments, not invented "viral scores", predicted CTR, or conversion probabilities. Stronger creative selection is a hypothesis to test, not a measured result.

## 5. Claim gates without flattening the writing

When a claim lacks support, change the persuasive mechanism instead of adding a long disclaimer to every hook. Product specificity and humor can remain sharp without fabricated proof.

| Claim type | What must exist | When missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-person usage, repeat purchase, regret, transformation | The actual speaker's supplied or verified experience, duration, and outcome | Use an observer, buyer question, or planned demo; do not pretend the creator tested it |
| "Viral", "everyone buys", professional adoption, popularity | Relevant, current supporting evidence with a defensible scope | Focus on usefulness or curiosity, not invented crowd behavior |
| Percentages, "10x", savings, durability, health or financial outcomes | Evidence that supports the exact claim and applicable conditions | State a demonstrated capability or comparison question instead; no unmeasured multiplier |
| Price, discount, "cheaper", value comparison | Current product/variant/market details; comparable scope; conditions | Omit the number or ask the user; "premium" is not proof of premium quality |
| Scarcity, countdown, price increase, last chance | Current, confirmed inventory/promotion details and deadline where applicable | Use immediate relevance, a fit check, or a demo CTA; never fabricate urgency |
| Before/after or competitor superiority | Comparable, honest conditions and observed results | Propose a test as a test, not a result; no staged proof presented as real |
| Number of functions, compatibility, safety | Supported specs and actual use conditions | Name only supported functions; do not suggest unsafe workarounds |

An existing person's honest first-person line can be edited without replacing "I" with sterile marketing language. Preserve the scope of their experience; never generalize one result to all buyers. For a fictional actor scenario, label it outside the script and do not present a fabricated testimonial as a ready-to-publish real endorsement.

Affiliate work must not use "not an ad", "not sponsored", or "bukan endorse" to conceal a material relationship. When applicable, provide a clear disclosure separately from the hook options and place it appropriately in the finished script/post; do not advise hiding it after "more" or at the end of a video. Match the actual relationship: a possible commission is not the same as a paid sponsorship. Do not add "at no extra cost" unless that is supported. Verify jurisdiction and platform requirements for a live compliance question; this guide does not certify legal compliance worldwide.

## 6. Hook, proof, benefit, action are different jobs

"Click the link" is usually a CTA, not a hook. Scarcity is an offer condition, not an all-purpose opening strategy. Comment questions should help viewers recognize a problem or decide on fit, not mechanically demand likes, tags, and shares.

For video/script requests, connect:

`first frame / spoken hook -> concrete demo or explanation -> supported benefit and limitation -> one suitable action`

Label a proposed shot as a shot to film, not footage already captured. A text-only hook must stand alone; a visually dependent hook should name the required shot when explaining options. Reveal the answer promptly rather than delaying until the end just to manufacture watch time. Never fabricate a comparison, customer message, review, or dashboard as proof.

Use one action matched to the audience's stage: inspect fit, watch the test, compare dimensions, open product details, or buy when appropriate. Avoid unrelated curiosity that attracts attention but gives no reason to care about the product. For sensitive products, do not let persuasive wording override applicable safety, advertising, or evidence requirements.

## 7. Output defaults

- **Hooks only / ready to copy:** return the requested number of clean lines. No formulas, IDs, CTA, hashtags, labels, or explanations inside the copy unless requested. Put any essential production caveat outside the lines.
- **Hooks + recommendation:** give the hooks, then identify up to three promising candidates with one concise, concrete reason each. Call these editorial picks, not predicted winners.
- **Video concepts:** give a hook, first-frame action, next beat/payoff, proof needed, and CTA. Do not imply rendering or filming happened.
- **Audit:** identify the weak mechanism or unsupported claim and replace it with a materially better angle; do not only swap synonyms.
- **Repair:** change only the requested hook or selected lines. Preserve locked facts, price, body, CTA, and tone constraints.
- **A/B plan:** isolate the opening as far as practical; keep offer, audience, body, and conditions comparable. Suggest measuring attention and downstream actions without inventing platform metric definitions, a universal sample size, or statistical significance.

Follow the user's latest language and tone requirements. Do not append `@btr` to finished sales copy. Do not publish, contact buyers, scrape customer data, or spend ad money merely because a hook is ready.
