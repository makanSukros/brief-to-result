# Ideas and strategy

Use these workflows when the requested result is a direction, opportunity, or experiment. Read only the relevant section. Follow the user's requested quantity, depth, and format instead of the defaults below. Distinguish facts, assumptions, and hypotheses. Use the actual brief for audience, market, locale, currency, and timezone; do not assume a country. Honor an explicit output language and the target audience's specified language. Preserve the language of existing text when editing unless translation is requested. Default to English only for newly drafted content with no language signal. Never infer market, locale, currency, date format, or timezone from English.

## I01 — Ten meaningfully different ideas

- **Trigger:** “Give me ideas,” “add more options,” or “avoid the usual suggestions.”
- **Input:** Required: a topic or problem. Optional: audience, goal, channel, budget, and approaches already tried. Use available context without demanding a complete brief.
- **Method:** Divide the opportunity space by customer need, usage situation, mechanism, and delivery format. Generate ten candidates that differ on at least two dimensions. Remove duplicates before choosing the three strongest options.
- **Output:** Concrete ideas with the intended beneficiary, reason for relevance, and first practical step; identify the strongest options and explain the selection.
- **Check:** Do not claim an idea is unprecedented without evidence. Avoid ten titles describing the same mechanism. Respect the user's requested quantity.
- **Example:** “Suggest ten onboarding ideas for a team management app; select three we can test inexpensively this week.”

## I02 — Message angle matrix

- **Trigger:** “Try another angle,” “the messaging feels repetitive,” or “explore more ways to present this.”
- **Input:** Required: a topic, offer, or core message. Optional: customer segments, audience awareness, objections, and channel.
- **Method:** Cross three or four audience needs with three message approaches, such as demonstrations, common mistakes, and changes in working habits. Fill the cells with specific concepts, then choose the most relevant and distinct combinations.
- **Output:** A compact matrix and five selected angles, each with a premise, sample opening, and evidence needed to support it.
- **Check:** Do not turn demographics into stereotypes or fill empty cells with unconfirmed product claims. Reduce the matrix when a smaller result meets the request.
- **Example:** “Develop message angles for an inventory app aimed at shops using paper records; go beyond saving time.”

## I03 — Discover user problems

- **Trigger:** “What is the real problem?”, “what do users actually need?”, or “identify their pain points.”
- **Input:** Required: a user group and work situation. Optional: interviews, complaints, activity recordings, or usage data.
- **Method:** Map the trigger for a task, desired outcome, current steps, obstacles, workarounds, and cost of the obstacles. Separate observations from guesses. Write interview questions about recent events instead of hypothetical willingness to buy.
- **Output:** Five prioritized problem hypotheses, the supporting evidence or evidence gaps, and questions or observations that could test each one.
- **Check:** Do not present a proposed feature as a proven need. Include evidence that could disprove a hypothesis. Never imply interviews have happened when they have not.
- **Example:** “Use these fifteen support complaints to identify course administrators' main problems and useful follow-up interview questions.”

## I04 — Define positioning

- **Trigger:** “What makes my product different?”, “how should we introduce it?”, or “give us stronger positioning.”
- **Input:** Required: a product and its confirmed capabilities. Optional: customer segment, current alternatives, evidence, and reasons customers buy.
- **Method:** Identify who benefits most, the situation creating demand, realistic alternatives, distinctive value, and reasons to believe it. Develop three positions with different customer or benefit priorities. Recommend one using the available evidence and business fit.
- **Output:** One positioning statement, three message pillars, a brief product introduction, and any important evidence still needed.
- **Check:** Do not confuse positioning with a slogan. Avoid unsupported claims such as “first,” “best,” or “only.” Separate available capabilities from planned features.
- **Example:** “Position a course platform with built-in live sessions for independent instructors; explain why they might change their current workflow.”

## I05 — Compare competitors using evidence

- **Trigger:** “Compare competitors,” “find a market gap,” or “where could we win?”
- **Input:** Required: the products or category and the decision this comparison should inform. Optional: named competitors, sources, target market, and user priorities.
- **Method:** Choose comparison dimensions from customers' actual tasks. Verify changing claims such as prices and features against available official sources, recording the check date. Compare capabilities, constraints, relevant costs, and switching effort. Mark unknown information explicitly.
- **Output:** An evidence-backed table, potentially meaningful gaps, and experiments to establish whether customers care about those gaps.
- **Check:** Do not conceal estimates in empty cells. Failure to find a feature does not prove it is absent. State limitations when source verification is unavailable.
- **Example:** “Compare these three email tools for a small retailer, focusing on automation and costs at 5,000 contacts.”

## I06 — Test an idea at low cost

- **Trigger:** “Validate this first,” “how do we know anyone wants it?”, or “test it before building everything.”
- **Input:** Required: an idea and its riskiest assumption. Optional: target customers, audience access, budget, time, and team capabilities.
- **Method:** Turn the assumption into a measurable prediction. Choose an experiment that observes behavior, such as a demonstration, manual service, or interest page with an honest availability description. Define recruitment, target behavior, decision thresholds, and duration before running it.
- **Output:** An experiment plan, minimum materials, measures, and explicit continue, change, or stop decisions for plausible results.
- **Check:** Distinguish interest, usage, and payment. Do not present proposed thresholds as industry standards. Do not imply an unavailable product already exists.
- **Example:** “Test instructors' interest in a course subscription package within seven days using a limited budget.”

## I07 — Prioritize opportunities

- **Trigger:** “What should we do first?”, “everything seems important,” or “choose the most promising opportunities.”
- **Input:** Required: a list of opportunities and a goal. Optional: capacity, dependencies, impact evidence, costs, and deadlines.
- **Method:** Select a few criteria that directly relate to the goal. Distinguish evidenced impact from confidence. Compare effort, learning speed, dependencies, and risk. Use scores only when their definitions and assumptions are explainable; check whether plausible assumption changes alter the ranking.
- **Output:** An ordered shortlist, reasons for the first choice, deferred opportunities, and one concrete starting action.
- **Check:** Avoid false numerical precision. Do not favor easy work that fails to advance the goal. Identify choices sensitive to missing information.
- **Example:** “Rank these five features for improving activation; two developers have two weeks available.”

## I08 — Model business scenarios

- **Trigger:** “Could this be profitable?”, “how should we monetize it?”, or “how many customers cover the costs?”
- **Input:** Required: a revenue model and usable costs or explicit assumptions. Optional: pricing, conversion, retention, service costs, support, payment fees, and capacity.
- **Method:** Separate revenue, variable costs per customer, and fixed costs. Build conservative, base, and optimistic scenarios with visible assumptions. Calculate contribution margin and applicable break-even volume; vary the most influential inputs. Verify current rates using available sources.
- **Output:** Calculations, formulas, assumptions, viability conditions, and the next data most worth collecting, using the brief's currency and period.
- **Check:** Label scenarios as scenarios. Distinguish revenue, profit, and cash flow. Do not calculate positive break-even volume from a nonpositive contribution margin.
- **Example:** “Model three usage scenarios for a live-class business, separating video delivery, support, and payment costs.”

## I09 — Plan a launch

- **Trigger:** “Build a launch plan,” “where do we start?”, or “prepare a realistic rollout.”
- **Input:** Required: a product or offer and the launch objective. Optional: dates, feature readiness, audience, team, budget, channels, and evidence of demand.
- **Method:** Define the initial segment and success action. Sequence product readiness, messaging, distribution, onboarding, support, and measurement. Place prerequisites before dependent work. Identify the smallest launch scope that can still fulfill the offer's promises.
- **Output:** A phased plan or schedule using known dates, role owners, phase deliverables, success measures, and responses to weak demand or exceeded capacity.
- **Check:** Do not invent official dates or assume a timezone. Distinguish preparation from publication. Avoid channels the team cannot realistically operate.
- **Example:** “Plan a feature launch to existing users with three team members and ten days of preparation.”

## I10 — Find blind spots

- **Trigger:** “What am I missing?”, “challenge this plan,” or “identify the blind spots.”
- **Input:** Required: a plan or decision. Optional: assumptions, constraints, evidence, stakeholders, and decisions already settled.
- **Method:** Imagine the plan has failed at a relevant future point. Trace causes across customer needs, distribution, costs, operations, dependencies, and customer experience. Prioritize by justified impact and likelihood. Separate concrete risks from generic possibilities with little relevance.
- **Output:** Five to seven major blind spots, early warning signs, preventive actions, and the most valuable questions. Ask questions progressively when user answers are needed.
- **Check:** Do not substitute a long questionnaire for analysis. Do not treat possible failure as inevitable. Identify evidence that would change the assessment.
- **Example:** “Challenge this course platform plan before we add a marketplace; focus on issues that could undermine the business.”
